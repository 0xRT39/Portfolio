Most of Boston's independent music shops have closed. Two have not: Mr. Music, open on Harvard Avenue in Allston since 1973, and The Music Emporium in Lexington, open since 1971. Both do things any business school would call bad practice. They welcome browsers who never buy, give away expert advice and let customers play the stock for as long as they like. I spent a semester working out why those habits keep them open, and what that means for designing services built on long relationships.

## Method

I used a different method each time, so each visit could test what the last one suggested.

1. **Observation.** 85 minutes of observation on a Saturday afternoon at Mr. Music, describing the layout, sounds and routines without interpreting them yet.
2. **Interviews and online research.** Five informal in-store interviews with staff, regulars, a newcomer and a browser. I also searched 20 years of public discussion across 8 platforms: Reddit, Yelp, Google Reviews and five musicians' forums.
3. **Comparison.** A same-day visit to The Music Emporium, as a comparison store. The two shops are about the same age and both independently owned. They differ in setting, specialty and style, so my sense of what was normal at Mr. Music became visible again.
4. **Literature.** Readings on credence goods, showrooming and cultivating customers, to name what the fieldwork kept showing.

In all, the fieldwork took **more than 7 hours over 3 site visits**. The final report runs to 4,600 words and draws on 15 sources.

## What I found

**An 8-step pattern that no sign enforces.** Almost every customer at Mr. Music followed the same sequence:

1. Pause at the door.
2. Scan the walls.
3. Drift to the electric or the acoustic side.
4. Take an instrument down.
5. Carry it to the amplifiers.
6. Sit on the stool.
7. Play quietly.
8. Buy it, or hang it back up.

Nothing in the shop asked for this. When someone broke the pattern, for example by playing at full volume, staff stepped in gently. The shop runs on unwritten rules that have taken decades to settle.

**Three problems the shops solve that a screen can't:**

- **Credence goods.** Buyers can't judge a used or vintage instrument's quality, even after playing it. A free 15-minute appraisal of an inherited Martin guitar, with no sale attempted, is how the shop earns the trust it relies on.
- **Showrooming.** Some products resist being bought online. A haggle over an amplifier was the shop working out, out loud, its place against online sellers.
- **Cultivation.** Customers are grown over years, not closed in minutes. The regular who visits weekly without buying is the shop's long bet on a future customer.

## Four implications for design

[![Slide: four implications. Formality is not the same as inclusion. Long horizons demand different metrics. The physical environment talks. Slowness is part of the methodology.](assets/projects/the-long-counter/four-implications.webp)](assets/projects/the-long-counter/four-implications.webp)

*The four implications, each drawn from both the fieldwork and the literature.*

1. **Formality is not the same as inclusion.** Mr. Music's unwritten rules left one newcomer unsure what she was allowed to do. The Emporium's more formal setting left a visitor in its doorway unsure whether he was the kind of person it was for. Making things formal swaps one kind of newcomer friction for another.
2. **Long horizons need different metrics.** Conversion rate is the wrong measure for a business whose customers take years to buy.
3. **The physical environment communicates.** Lighting, how crowded the shelves are, sound, and whether you can see into the back room all tell people how to behave.
4. **Slowness is part of the method.** Each visit broadened the methods, and together they produced insights no single method would have.

## Results

- I coded **230 field-note and interview excerpts and 180 online posts** into 11 themes. A peer double-coded 20% of them, with agreement of **κ = 0.81**.
- I turned the second implication into a **5-measure relationship-health scorecard** for businesses with long customer horizons. The measures are visit frequency, advice given per sale, trial-to-purchase time, how many browsers become buyers, and repeat customers' share of sales.

## Why it matters for UX

Most product metrics reward speed: fewer steps, faster conversion. This project is my reminder that some experiences work because they are slow. The research job is to tell which kind you are designing for before you optimize it.
