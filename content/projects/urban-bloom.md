Urban Bloom is a nonprofit concept for UN Sustainable Development Goal 11, sustainable cities and communities. It turns underused city land, such as empty lots, old gas stations, warehouses and land bought out after natural disasters, into community gardens. It is aimed at budget-stretched families in food deserts, who have little fresh produce nearby. I developed it with three classmates through three design reviews over one semester.

## Mapping the system first

Before designing any service, we mapped everyone a community garden depends on.

- **Version 1** captured every possible connection, and nobody could read it.
- **Version 2** grouped the stakeholders, but the groups were still too broad.
- **Version 3** arranges them in rings by how directly they are involved:
  - **Direct:** workers, volunteers, donors and community members.
  - **Secondary:** energy companies, restaurants and experts.
  - **Tertiary:** the real-estate market and supply manufacturers.

The rings showed where partnerships would give us the most for the least effort. We followed the map with a social innovation canvas and a business model canvas, each with a social layer and an environmental layer.

## The service

The blueprint follows five touchpoints:

1. Building the garden
2. Upkeep
3. Composting
4. Educational classes
5. Receiving produce

For each touchpoint it shows four layers: what families do, what garden staff do in front of them, what happens behind the scenes, and the support processes run by the parent nonprofit. Food waste goes back into the gardens as compost, closing the loop.

[![Service blueprint with photo strips for each touchpoint and swimlanes for customer actions, frontstage, backstage and support processes.](assets/projects/urban-bloom/service-blueprint.webp)](assets/projects/urban-bloom/service-blueprint.webp)

*The service blueprint. The line of visibility separates what families see from what the nonprofit coordinates behind it.*

A life-cycle assessment traced **90% of the expected carbon emissions to three stages**:

- **Production:** site preparation and building by hand.
- **Materials:** reclaimed wood and preserved seed.
- **Distribution:** renting equipment locally instead of buying it.

Those became the stages we designed to cut emissions.

## Making it fundable

We modeled a **$45K launch budget** against **$52K in projected first-year revenue** from five streams:

- Major donors: $30K
- Government grants: $10K
- Small donors: $5K
- Events and workshops: $5K
- Merchandise: $2K

[![Two slides: the $45,000 initial investment broken down by category, and five revenue streams totalling $52,000.](assets/projects/urban-bloom/budget-and-revenue.webp)](assets/projects/urban-bloom/budget-and-revenue.webp)

*Launch budget and revenue streams.*

The rollout runs in five phases over five years:

1. Months 1–3: plan.
2. Months 3–6: engage local government.
3. Months 6–12: open the first site.
4. Year 2: write the playbook for expansion.
5. Years 3–5: set up a national governing body and expand to other cities.

## Results

- I tested the concept with 14 residents of two food-desert neighborhoods and 3 community-garden coordinators. **12 of 14 residents said they would join in the first season.**
- Walking the coordinators through the blueprint surfaced **9 backstage gaps**, such as tool storage, volunteer insurance and produce hand-off times. We closed all of them in the final version.
- On the modeled numbers, the first garden **covers its costs in year one with a $7K surplus**.
